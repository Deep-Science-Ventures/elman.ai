Thesis

Our thesis

Therapeutic development will become predictable when we can learn which evidence actually translates to humans. The missing training data is the link between evidence available at a decision, the judgement it justified, and what happened next. Elman is building that dataset and the model that learns from it.

Everywhere else in AI, scale has won when the right training signal could be generated or collected. Vision, language, code, game play and protein structure all benefited from large numbers of examples where the input and the thing being predicted were closely aligned, or where failure could be generated cheaply through simulation, self-play or repeated experimentation.

Therapeutic development is different. Human outcomes are sparse, slow and expensive, and most of the evidence available before them is not the human outcome itself but a proxy for it: a mouse that is not a human, an organoid that is neither, a cell measured outside its niche, a molecular readout several biological layers away from the patient. There is enormous biological data, but very little labelled experience telling us how far each kind of evidence should carry toward the human answer.

The data required to learn that translation has therefore never been collected systematically. A fully representative dataset would require measuring DNA, RNA, protein, cell, tissue, organ and whole-system biology inside living humans, across interventions and outcomes, at the breadth required to cover therapeutic development. That is impossible. What exists instead is fragmented evidence distributed across those layers, each source informative but imperfect in a different way. Roughly 90% of drugs that enter clinical trials never reach approval[1]. The causes are many, from efficacy to safety to commercial choice. The problem is not that the available proxies are useless. It is that their relationship to the eventual human outcome has never been measured systematically.

The first step is to make that translation problem machine-readable. The Judgement Engine extracts experimental evidence before it reasons over it, turning individual results into a shared, provenance-tracked representation: what was tested, in which biological context, with what intervention, effect, statistics and source. It connects those results into evidence graphs spanning biological layers, then uses them to make explicit judgements about what the evidence supports, where an argument breaks and what additional evidence would change it.

That creates something conventional scientific AI systems do not naturally preserve: the state of the evidence at the moment a therapeutic judgement is made. Once that judgement is linked to the experiment, programme decision or clinical outcome that follows, it becomes a labelled example of how far that evidence actually carried.

This is also why better frontier models are a tailwind rather than an existential threat. Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and specialist scientific models will continue to improve literature search, data analysis, coding, hypothesis generation and scientific agents. Elman is model-agnostic and can route those capabilities as they improve.

But scaling those models on existing biological data does not, by itself, solve the translation problem. The underlying data remain sparse and misaligned across biological layers: better prediction of a molecular state does not tell you how faithfully it carries into a cell, tissue, organism or patient. The missing supervision is the connection between evidence available at a real decision, the judgement justified by that evidence, and what happened afterwards. Creating that dataset requires deploying against consequential therapeutic questions, preserving what was knowable at the time and collecting downstream experimental, programme and clinical outcomes. Elman is organised around producing exactly that supervision.

With the Allen Institute, Elman has already demonstrated the first half of this system. Their multi-region Alzheimer’s atlas revealed an unexpected human observation: a population of visual-cortex neurons is selectively vulnerable while closely related neighbouring neurons are relatively spared. The atlas showed what was happening; it did not contain the experimental evidence required to explain why.

Elman went beyond the atlas. It generated competing explanations and tested them against experimental results extracted from the scientific literature and complementary analyses. In the resulting workflow, ~170,000 experimental facts were structured into a common evidential representation and 225 hypotheses were investigated. 51% of hypotheses generated by the initial state-of-the-art multi-agent workflow were disproven by Elman validation on the same evidence, with 95% agreement with expert review. The surviving evidence converged on hyperexcitability, with receptor balance, calcium handling and ion-channel activity providing mechanistic routes to a specific physiological prediction. That work is now in a paper submitted to Cell, with the preprint public[2], and the findings inspectable here.

The next step is to learn the translation itself. We are pairing time-locked evidence graphs with historical clinical outcomes: reconstructing only the evidence available before each programme or trial read out, then asking Elman to predict what will survive in humans and why. The outcome is hidden during prediction and revealed only for evaluation, with held-out and prospective readouts used to test generalisation.

The objective is not one universal accuracy number. Translation depends on context. We want to quantify where Elman is most predictive — by therapeutic area, modality, evidence regime and decision type — and what kinds of evidence are required for a judgement to deserve confidence. Those outcome-linked examples then become the training data for models that learn which patterns in the evidence graph predict successful translation.

Clinical trials provide the most consequential labels, but they are not the only ones. Wet-lab experiments and programme outcomes can return faster, narrower signals along the way. Over time, every real deployment can add another example of evidence at decision time → judgement → outcome, building the dataset therapeutic development has never systematically collected.
